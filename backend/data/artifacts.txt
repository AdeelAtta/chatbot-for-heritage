# Artifacts of Mohenjo-daro

## Overview

The artifacts recovered from Mohenjo-daro provide invaluable insights into the daily life, artistic achievements, technological capabilities, and cultural practices of the Indus Valley Civilization. These objects, spanning categories from everyday tools to exquisite works of art, collectively paint a vivid picture of a sophisticated Bronze Age society that rivaled ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia in its achievements.

Excavations at Mohenjo-daro have produced tens of thousands of artifacts, ranging from mass-produced utilitarian objects to rare masterpieces of ancient art. These items are preserved in museums across Pakistan, India, and around the world, though significant collections remain in Karachi and Lahore.

## Indus Seals

### Description and Characteristics

Indus seals represent one of the most characteristic and enigmatic artifact categories from Mohenjo-daro. These small carved tablets, typically measuring 2-3 centimeters square, were crafted primarily from steatite (soapstone) though examples in other materials have been found.

Each seal typically features two elements: an inscription in the undeciphered Indus script running along the top or in a central band, and a carved relief depicting an animal or other motif below. The most common motif features a single-horned bovine animal, sometimes called a "unicorn," often shown in a distinctive profile view with a circular altar or ritual object in front.

### Functions and Uses

The precise function of Indus seals remains debated, but several theories have been proposed:

Trade Authorization: The uniformity of seal designs and the consistent script may have served to authorize commercial transactions, similar to merchant stamps in other ancient trading systems.

Property Marking: Seals may have been used to mark ownership of goods, containers, or property.

Identity Tokens: The script and motifs may have identified individual merchants, guilds, or commercial organizations.

Ritual Objects: Some seals show signs of ritual use including burning marks and wear patterns inconsistent with commercial stamping.

Status Symbols: The quality of seal craftsmanship and the materials used may have indicated the owner's social status.

### The Undeciphered Script

The inscriptions on Indus seals and other artifacts constitute our primary evidence for the Harappan writing system. With over 400 distinct signs identified, the script is considered logo-syllabic, combining logographic and syllabic elements similar to Egyptian hieroglyphics or Sumerian cuneiform.

Despite numerous attempts, the script remains undeciphered. The brevity of inscriptions, typically containing only 5-10 signs, limits contextual analysis. Without decipherment, we cannot read administrative records, religious texts, or personal communications, significantly limiting our understanding of Harappan society.

### Notable Seal Discoveries

Among the thousands of seals found at Mohenjo-daro, several deserve special mention:

The Unicorn Seal series represents the most common motif, with variations suggesting different ownership or administrative functions.

The "King" or "Chief" seal, featuring a larger format and more elaborate carving, possibly indicates high administrative status.

Seals depicting human figures in various poses, providing rare visual documentation of Harappan dress and appearance.

Seals with unusual motifs or script variations that may indicate special functions or regional variations.

## Bronze Sculptures

### The Dancing Girl

The Dancing Girl is perhaps the most celebrated artifact from Mohenjo-daro and ranks among the finest bronze sculptures of the ancient world. Discovered in 1926 during excavations at the site, this 14.2-centimeter-tall statue depicts a young woman in a confident, dynamic pose.

The figure stands with her left hand on her hip and her right arm adorned with multiple bangles extending from wrist to shoulder. Her hair, gathered in an elaborate style with numerous braids, falls across her left shoulder. The confident posture and knowing expression suggest a dancer, actress, or possibly a ritual performer.

Technical analysis indicates the statue was created using the lost-wax casting technique, with the hollow bronze shell preserved to this day in remarkable detail. The naturalistic proportions and sensitive observation of human anatomy demonstrate sophisticated understanding of the human form.

The statue is now housed at the National Museum of Pakistan in Karachi, where it serves as a symbol of Pakistan's ancient cultural heritage.

### The Priest King

The so-called Priest King is a steatite bust discovered at Mohenjo-daro depicting a male figure wearing an elaborate headband and possibly a decorated robe. Standing approximately 17.5 centimeters tall, this carved sculpture represents one of the finest examples of Harappan stone carving.

The figure wears a headband with a circular ornament at the front, possibly representing authority or divine sanction. His eyes appear half-closed as if in meditation or contemplation. The trefoil pattern decorating his robe may have ritual significance.

The interpretation of this figure remains debated. The term "Priest King" reflects early assumptions about Harappan governance, but the figure may represent a deity, ritual specialist, or person of high status engaged in ceremonial activities.

The bust is preserved at the National Museum of Pakistan in Karachi.

### Other Bronze Objects

Beyond the celebrated sculptures, Mohenjo-daro has produced numerous bronze artifacts including:

Bronze mirrors with reflective surfaces created through careful polishing of copper-bronze alloys.

Bronze weapons including arrowheads, spearheads, and axes, demonstrating metallurgical and craft production capabilities.

Bronze tools including axes, saws, and other implements indicating advanced metalworking technology.

Bronze jewelry in various forms including bangles, rings, and small decorative items.

## Terracotta Figurines

### Female Figurines

Terracotta female figurines represent one of the most common artifact categories at Mohenjo-daro. These mass-produced items typically feature:

Prominent breasts and hips, possibly indicating fertility symbolism or pregnancy.

Elaborate hairstyles often featuring multiple braids or head ornaments.

Large jewelry pieces including necklaces, earrings, and armbands rendered in applied clay.

Headdresses, sometimes shaped into distinctive forms suggesting ceremonial significance.

Red pigment, preserved in some examples, indicating painted decoration.

The frequency and standardization of these figurines suggest they served religious or ritual purposes, possibly representing a fertility goddess or mother figure worshipped by the Harappan civilization.

### Male Figurines

Male terracotta figurines are less common and typically more standardized than female forms. These figures often depict:

Standing male figures with detailed facial features rendered in clay.

Figures wearing turbans or other head coverings.

Figures holding objects in raised hands, possibly indicating blessing, prayer, or triumph.

Figures with elaborate body ornamentation suggesting ritual rather than everyday dress.

### Animal Figurines

Terracotta animal figurines represent another major category, including:

Bull figurines in various poses, possibly associated with the bull motifs on seals.

Elephant and rhinoceros figures reflecting the wildlife of the region.

Monkey figurines, sometimes shown in human-like poses.

Bird figurines, possibly representing doves, peacocks, or other local species.

Dogs and other domestic animals.

### Toys and Games

Children's toys represent a significant subset of terracotta artifacts:

Toy carts with wheels, pulled by or pushed by figurines.

Animal figurines with holes suggesting they were mounted on sticks or poles.

Whistles shaped like birds, humans, or geometric forms.

Small vessels in miniature form, possibly for children's play.

## Jewelry and Personal Adornment

### Gold Objects

Gold jewelry from Mohenjo-daro demonstrates sophisticated metallurgical skills:

Gold necklaces composed of various bead types including terracotta, steatite, and faience covered in thin gold foil.

Gold bangles in various forms, from simple circles to elaborately decorated bands.

Gold ear ornaments including hoops and pendant styles.

Gold hairpins with decorative finials.

The gold used in Mohenjo-daro jewelry appears to have originated from various sources including Afghanistan and the Himalayan regions, demonstrating extensive trade networks.

### Semi-Precious Stones

Jewelry incorporating semi-precious stones demonstrates the Harappan mastery of lapidary arts:

Carnelian beads from Gujarat, with characteristic red-orange coloring, were among the most valued trade items. The distinctive barrel and cylindrical shapes were produced using sophisticated drilling techniques.

Lapis lazuli, traded from deposits in Afghanistan, was worked into beads, inlays, and small decorative items.

Agate, found in various local deposits, was worked into numerous decorative forms.

Jasper, available from regional sources, provided additional colors for jewelry production.

### Faience

Faience, a glazed ceramic material produced in various blue-green colors, was extensively used for jewelry:

Faience beads in numerous shapes were among the most common decorative items.

Bangles made from faience represent significant production achievements.

Figurines and small decorative objects demonstrate the versatility of the material.

### Shell and Bone

Materials from marine and animal sources provided additional jewelry-making opportunities:

Shell bangles, made from Conus shell, were popular personal ornaments.

Bone implements and decorations were commonly used in domestic contexts.

Ivory items, including beads and inlays, demonstrate trade connections with regions supporting elephant populations.

## Pottery

### Fine Ware

Fine pottery with polished red surfaces represents one of the hallmark ceramic products of Mohenjo-daro:

Red-slipped pottery with black-painted geometric decorations represents the finest quality tableware.

The consistency of forms and decoration across the Harappan territory suggests standardized production methods.

Storage vessels with capacities suggesting specific uses in household and commercial contexts.

### utilitarian Ware

Coarse pottery served utilitarian purposes:

Cooking vessels with thick walls designed to withstand direct heat.

Storage jars of various sizes for water, grain, and other household goods.

Mortars and grinding vessels for food processing.

Strainers and funnels indicating complex cooking practices.

### Standardization

The remarkable standardization of pottery forms across Mohenjo-daro and other Harappan sites indicates coordinated production under some form of quality control. Wheel-thrown pottery using consistent techniques and clay recipes suggests organized workshop production.

## Tools and Weapons

### Stone Tools

Despite the presence of copper-bronze metallurgy, stone tools continued to be produced:

Ground stone axes and celts for woodworking and other tasks.

Stone querns and grinding stones for food processing.

Flint and other stone blades for cutting tasks.

### Copper-Bronze Objects

Metallurgical artifacts demonstrate advanced technology:

Axes in various forms including flat axes, shouldered axes, and socketed axes.

Swords and daggers with distinctive Harappan blade shapes.

Spearheads and arrowheads in standardized forms.

Fishhooks, awls, and other small implements.

The presence of both cast and forged objects indicates sophisticated metallurgical knowledge.

### Weights

Standardized weights represent one of the Harappan civilization's most important innovations:

Weights follow binary and decimal progressions, allowing accurate measurement for trade.

The consistency of weights across the vast Harappan territory indicates standardized commercial practices.

Stone weights were carefully calibrated and show signs of long use, suggesting they served as reference standards.

## Special Artifacts

### Masks

A small number of terracotta masks have been found at Mohenjo-daro, representing rare and possibly ritual objects:

Human-faced masks with holes for attachment suggest they were worn or displayed in ceremonial contexts.

The masks often feature exaggerated features and elaborate decoration.

### Musical Instruments

Limited evidence for musical instruments includes:

Clay whistles, possibly used as toys or ritual items.

Bone flutes with finger holes suggesting musical function.

Tambourines or rattles indicated by hollow forms with contents suggesting percussion.

### Games

Board games represented another category of artifacts:

Twenty-squares game boards carved into stone or terracotta.

Dice made from various materials.

Game tokens in various shapes and materials.

## Preservation and Display

### Current Locations

Major collections of Mohenjo-daro artifacts are preserved at:

National Museum of Pakistan, Karachi - houses the Dancing Girl, Priest King, and numerous other major pieces.

Larkana Museum, Sindh - regional collection with artifacts from the Sindh province, where Mohenjo-daro is located.

Archaeological Museum, Mohenjo-daro - site museum with local discoveries available for visitors to the site near Larkana.

Lahore Museum - significant Harappan collection.

Pakistan National Museum of Anthropological Sciences, Karachi - extensive collection of artifacts and documentation.

Museums in India including the National Museum, New Delhi, and various state museums.

International collections include items at the British Museum, London, and other major institutions.

### Conservation Challenges

The preservation of Mohenjo-daro artifacts presents significant challenges:

Organic materials rarely survive due to the water table and humidity conditions.

Bronze disease, a destructive chemical process, threatens metal artifacts.

Terracotta pieces can be damaged by salt crystallization as sites dry out.

Steatite objects are relatively stable but can be damaged by handling and environmental changes.

Modern conservation efforts focus on controlled environments, specialized storage, and careful handling protocols.
