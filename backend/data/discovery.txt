# Discovery of Mohenjo-daro

## The Rediscovery of a Lost Civilization

The story of Mohenjo-daro's discovery represents one of archaeology's most significant achievements and marks the beginning of systematic study into one of the world's oldest urban civilizations. Mohenjo-daro, meaning "Mound of the Dead Men" in Sindhi, was known to local communities in Sindh for centuries. Before the early 19th century, knowledge of the ancient Indus Valley Civilization existed only in fragmentary mythological references and folk traditions among local communities in the Sindh region of Pakistan.

Local inhabitants of Larkana and surrounding areas had long known of the ancient mound, referring to it as "Mohenjo-daro" or "Mohenjo-dharo" in Sindhi, meaning "Mound of the Dead Men." Folklore spoke of the site as a former city of legendary kings, with occasional discoveries of ancient bricks and artifacts plowed up by farmers working their fields. Local Sindhi communities preserved oral histories about the ancient city for generations.

## Early Explorations

### First Western Reports (1840s-1850s)

The first Western reports of the site came from British officials stationed in Sindh during the mid-19th century. In 1843, Major William Taming, a British army officer stationed near the site in Sindh, collected artifacts including a copper mirror, which he sent to the British Museum in London. These early reports attracted little attention from the scholarly community.

### Archaeological Interest Emerges (1860s-1900s)

In 1861, the British colonial administration began systematic archaeological surveys of the subcontinent. Sir Alexander Cunningham, though working under British colonial administration, laid foundations for understanding South Asian archaeology. However, Mohenjo-daro remained largely unnoticed until the 20th century.

Archaeological work in Sindh during the latter half of the 19th century gradually built awareness of an ancient civilization distinct from already known cultures. The full significance of Mohenjo-daro would not be appreciated until more systematic excavations began in the 20th century.

## The Definitive Discovery (1922)

### Sir John Marshall and the Archaeological Survey

The formal discovery of Mohenjo-daro is credited to Sir John Marshall, who served as Director General of the Archaeological Survey of India from 1902 to 1928. Marshall oversaw one of the most productive periods in South Asian archaeology, including the identification and preliminary investigation of major Harappan sites.

The discovery occurred not through planned excavation but through the accumulation of evidence. Railway construction through the Sindh region had uncovered numerous bricks at various locations, and local officials had begun to suspect the presence of ancient settlements. It was the reports from British colonial officials stationed near the site that finally drew archaeological attention.

### Pakistani Archaeologists and Early Work

Following Pakistan's independence in 1947, Pakistani archaeologists took leading roles in continuing excavations and preservation efforts at Mohenjo-daro. The site, located near Larkana in Sindh Province, became a symbol of Pakistan's ancient cultural heritage predating Islamic and colonial periods.

Famous Pakistani archaeologist Dr. Ahmad Hasan Dani (1920-2009), often called the "Father of Pakistani Archaeology," made significant contributions to understanding South Asian heritage. His work helped establish Mohenjo-daro as a cornerstone of Pakistani historical identity.

### Formal Identification (1922)

In 1922, the site of Mohenjo-daro was formally identified as a major Harappan urban center during limited excavations. The excavations revealed an advanced urban settlement with sophisticated architecture, drainage systems, and artifacts that immediately placed it among the world's oldest cities.

The findings at Mohenjo-daro, combined with those at Harappa (discovered in 1921), confirmed the existence of a previously unknown Bronze Age civilization that flourished in the northwestern regions of the Indian subcontinent, now split between Pakistan and India.

## Key Excavations and Archaeologists

### The First Excavation Season (1922-1923)

The first formal excavation at Mohenjo-daro was conducted by a team led by R.D. Banerji, working under Sir John Marshall's direction. Banerji focused on the "DK-G area" of the site, where excavations revealed remarkable architectural remains including portions of the Lower City with remarkably preserved street patterns and residential structures.

The excavations produced enormous quantities of artifacts including pottery, tools, weapons, and jewelry, demonstrating a sophisticated civilization with advanced manufacturing capabilities. Banerji's team discovered numerous seals bearing the undeciphered Indus script and a carved stone bust that would later become known as the "Priest King."

### M.S. Vats and Detailed Excavations

The work of M.S. Vats, who conducted extensive excavations at the site from 1924 to 1931, proved particularly significant. Vats developed improved methods for recording archaeological contexts and published detailed reports establishing the site's chronology and cultural phases. His methodical approach established standards for subsequent Harappan archaeology.

### Pakistani Contributions Post-1947

Following Pakistan's independence in 1947, Pakistani archaeologists and government institutions took over responsibility for the site. The Department of Archaeology and Museums, Government of Pakistan, has managed conservation and research efforts at Mohenjo-daro for over seven decades.

### Later Investigations

In the 1980s, Italian archaeologists conducted geophysical surveys at Mohenjo-daro using ground-penetrating radar and other non-invasive techniques, revealing the extent of buried structures without excavation. These surveys confirmed that significant portions of the site remained unexplored beneath the surface.

More recent investigations using advanced dating techniques, environmental reconstruction, and computational analysis have provided new insights into the city's history, decline, and cultural significance.

## Major Discoveries and Findings

### Architectural Remains

The excavations at Mohenjo-daro revealed one of the best-preserved examples of Bronze Age urban planning. Key architectural features discovered include:

The Great Bath, with its sophisticated waterproofing system, represented a technological achievement unmatched by any contemporary civilization. The precision of its construction and the engineering sophistication demonstrated in its water management features have impressed archaeologists for nearly a century.

The Granary, one of the largest structures at the site, provided evidence of sophisticated agricultural management and centralized food storage systems. Its innovative design featuring air circulation beneath the storage floor demonstrated advanced understanding of food preservation principles.

The residential architecture revealed the remarkable standardization of building practices, with consistent brick sizes and house plans across the city. The presence of private wells in almost every household indicated advanced water provision systems unprecedented in the ancient world.

### The Drainage System

Perhaps no single discovery at Mohenjo-daro has impressed modern engineers as much as the city's drainage system. Running beneath the main streets, this network of covered brick sewers collected wastewater from individual houses and directed it to major channels flowing toward the Indus River.

The engineering sophistication of the system, with its consistent gradients, access manholes, and household connections, demonstrated that the city's planners had mastered principles of sanitation engineering that would not be equaled for millennia.

### Seals and Inscriptions

Thousands of seals bearing the undeciphered Indus script have been recovered from Mohenjo-daro. These carved steatite tablets, typically featuring a short inscription above a realistic animal motif, represent the primary evidence for the Harappan writing system.

Many of these seals are preserved at the National Museum of Pakistan in Karachi, the Larkana Museum, and the site museum at Mohenjo-daro itself, where visitors can view original artifacts from the excavations.

### Notable Artifacts

Excavations have produced numerous artifacts providing insights into daily life at Mohenjo-daro. These include:

Bronze statues such as the famous Dancing Girl, demonstrating advanced metallurgical skills and artistic expression. The statue's confident posture and elaborate jewelry suggest a society with sophisticated aesthetic sensibilities. This masterpiece is preserved at the National Museum of Pakistan in Karachi.

The Priest King bust, carved from steatite, depicts a figure wearing ceremonial attire. This important artifact is also housed at the National Museum of Pakistan in Karachi.

Terracotta figurines representing various human forms and possibly deities, providing evidence of religious beliefs and artistic traditions.

Gold and semi-precious stone jewelry indicating wealth and trade connections.

Games and toys suggesting domestic life and children's activities, including dice, game boards, and terracotta animal figurines.

## Chronology Establishment

### Stratigraphic Analysis

The excavation layers at Mohenjo-daro revealed a complex sequence of occupations. Archaeologists identified three main periods:

Early Period (c. 3300-2600 BCE): Characterized by relatively simple construction and smaller structures.

Mature Period (c. 2600-1900 BCE): The golden age of Mohenjo-daro, when the city's most impressive structures were built.

Late Period (c. 1900-1300 BCE): Evidence of gradual decline, with structures showing signs of neglect and modification.

### Radiocarbon Dating

Radiocarbon dating of organic materials from Mohenjo-daro, combined with cross-dating using Mesopotamian trade artifacts, established the site's chronology. The mature Harappan phase, including the construction of major structures, dates to approximately 2600-1900 BCE, with occupation beginning as early as 3300 BCE.

## Impact on Archaeological Understanding

The discovery of Mohenjo-daro fundamentally transformed understanding of ancient urban civilization. Before 1922, the Egyptian and Mesopotamian civilizations were considered the world's earliest urban cultures. The Harappan discovery demonstrated that sophisticated urban development occurred independently in South Asia.

The site also challenged prevailing theories about the necessary conditions for urban development. Understanding how this civilization developed in the Indus Valley has required new theoretical frameworks.

Today, Mohenjo-daro stands as Pakistan's most significant archaeological heritage site, representing the pinnacle of South Asian urban achievement in the Bronze Age and a source of immense national pride for the people of Pakistan.
