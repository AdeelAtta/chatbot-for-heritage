# Discovery of Mohenjo-daro

## The Rediscovery of a Lost Civilization

The story of Mohenjo-daro's discovery represents one of archaeology's most significant achievements and marks the beginning of systematic study into one of the world's oldest urban civilizations. Before the early 19th century, knowledge of the Indus Valley Civilization existed only in fragmentary mythological references and folk traditions among local communities in Sindh and Punjab.

Local inhabitants of the region had long known of the ancient mound, referring to it as "Mohenjo-daro" or "Mound of the Dead Men." Folklore spoke of the site as a former city of king "Raor" or "Mohan" who ruled before the flood, with occasional discoveries of bricks and occasional artifacts plowed up by farmers working their fields.

## Early Explorations

### First Western Reports (1840s-1850s)

The first Western reports of the site came from British officials stationed in Sindh during the mid-19th century. In 1843, Major William Taming, a British army officer, was posted near the site and reportedly collected some artifacts including a copper mirror, which he sent to the British Museum in London. However, these early reports attracted little attention from the scholarly community.

Charles Masson, a British officer who had deserted from the East India Company army, explored numerous sites in Balochistan and Sindh between 1827 and 1838. Though he visited various ancient locations, his extensive journals contain no reference to Mohenjo-daro, suggesting the site had not yet attracted significant attention among antiquarians.

### Archaeological Interest Emerges (1860s-1900s)

In 1861, the newly established British Raj appointed Sir Alexander Cunningham as the first Director General of the Archaeological Survey of India. Cunningham and his successors conducted extensive surveys across the Indian subcontinent, identifying numerous ancient sites including several initially thought to be Harappan. However, Mohenjo-daro remained largely unnoticed in their reports.

Archaeological work at sites in Punjab and Sindh during the latter half of the 19th century gradually built awareness of an ancient civilization distinct from the already known cultures of Egypt and Mesopotamia. However, the full significance of these sites would not be appreciated until more systematic excavations began in the 20th century.

## The Definitive Discovery (1922)

### Sir John Marshall and the Archaeological Survey

The formal discovery of Mohenjo-daro is credited to Sir John Marshall, who served as Director General of the Archaeological Survey of India from 1902 to 1928. Marshall oversaw one of the most productive periods in Indian archaeology, including the identification and preliminary investigation of major Harappan sites.

The discovery occurred not through planned excavation but through the accumulation of evidence. Railway construction through the Sindh region had uncovered numerous bricks at various locations, and local officials had begun to suspect the presence of ancient settlements. However, it was the reports from British colonial officials stationed near the site that finally drew archaeological attention.

### D.R. Bhandarkar's Report

In 1912, a significant find occurred when a collection of seals was purchased from a antiquity dealer in Lahore. These seals bore inscriptions in an unknown script, immediately attracting scholarly interest. R.G. Bhandarkar, a noted archaeologist, reported on these finds and suggested they represented a civilization older than the established historical record for the subcontinent.

The 1912 discovery proved crucial in establishing interest in the broader region. Scholars began systematic searches for additional sites with similar characteristics, leading to excavations at Harappa in 1921 and the formal identification of Mohenjo-daro shortly thereafter.

### Formal Identification (1922)

In 1922, the site of Mohenjo-daro was formally identified as a major Harappan urban center during limited excavations conducted under the direction of Sir John Marshall. The excavations revealed an advanced urban settlement with sophisticated architecture, drainage systems, and artifacts that immediately placed it among the world's oldest cities.

The findings at Mohenjo-daro, combined with those at Harappa (discovered in 1921), confirmed the existence of a previously unknown Bronze Age civilization that flourished in the northwestern regions of the Indian subcontinent. This discovery fundamentally altered understanding of early human urban development and demonstrated that complex urban societies emerged independently in multiple world regions.

## Key Excavations and Archaeologists

### The First Excavation Season (1922-1923)

The first formal excavation at Mohenjo-daro was conducted by a team led by R.D. Banerji, working under Sir John Marshall's direction. Banerji focused on the so-called "DK-G area" of the site, where his excavations revealed remarkable architectural remains including portions of the Lower City with remarkably preserved street patterns and residential structures.

The excavations produced enormous quantities of artifacts including pottery, tools, weapons, and jewelry, all demonstrating a sophisticated civilization with advanced manufacturing capabilities. Most significantly, Banerji's team discovered numerous seals bearing the undeciphered Indus script and a carved stone bust that would later become known as the "Priest King."

### Subsequent Excavation Seasons

Following the initial discoveries, excavations at Mohenjo-daro continued intermittently through the 1920s and 1930s, conducted by various teams including archaeologists from the Archaeological Survey of India and foreign institutions. These subsequent excavations expanded understanding of the site's layout and chronology.

The work of M.S. Vats, who conducted extensive excavations at the site from 1924 to 1931, proved particularly significant. Vats developed improved methods for recording archaeological contexts and published detailed reports establishing the site's chronology and cultural phases. His methodical approach established standards for subsequent Harappan archaeology.

### Later Investigations

After World War II, excavations at Mohenjo-daro were limited, with most work focused on conservation and site management rather than new discoveries. Archaeological investigation shifted to understanding the broader Harappan civilization through regional surveys and excavations at other sites including Dholavira, Rakhigarhi, and numerous smaller settlements.

In the 1980s, Italian archaeologists conducted geophysical surveys at Mohenjo-daro using ground-penetrating radar and other non-invasive techniques, revealing the extent of buried structures without excavation. These surveys confirmed that significant portions of the site remained unexplored beneath the surface.

More recent investigations using advanced dating techniques, environmental reconstruction, and computational analysis have provided new insights into the city's history, decline, and cultural significance.

## Major Discoveries and Findings

### Architectural Remains

The excavations at Mohenjo-daro revealed one of the best-preserved examples of Bronze Age urban planning. Key architectural features discovered include:

The Great Bath, with its sophisticated waterproofing system, represented a technological achievement unmatched by any contemporary civilization. The precision of its construction and the engineering sophistication demonstrated in its water management features have impressed archaeologists for nearly a century.

The Granary, one of the largest structures at the site, provided evidence of sophisticated agricultural management and centralized food storage systems. Its innovative design featuring air circulation beneath the storage floor demonstrated advanced understanding of food preservation principles.

The residential architecture revealed the remarkable standardization of building practices, with consistent brick sizes and house plans across the city. The presence of private wells in almost every household indicated advanced water provision systems unprecedented in the ancient world.

### The Drainage System

Perhaps no single discovery at Mohenjo-daro has impressed modern engineers as much as the city's drainage system. Running beneath the main streets, this network of covered brick sewers collected wastewater from individual houses and directed it to major channels flowing toward the river.

The engineering sophistication of the system, with its consistent gradients, access manholes, and household connections, demonstrated that the city's planners had mastered principles of sanitation engineering that would not be equaled for millennia.

### Seals and Inscriptions

Thousands of seals bearing the undeciphered Indus script have been recovered from Mohenjo-daro. These carved steatite tablets, typically featuring a short inscription above a realistic animal motif, represent the primary evidence for the Harappan writing system.

The uniformity of the seal designs across the vast Harappan territory, combined with the script's undeciphered status, continues to make these artifacts objects of intense scholarly interest. Each attempt to decode the script potentially offers insights into governance, religion, trade, and social organization.

### Notable Artifacts

Excavations have produced numerous artifacts providing insights into daily life at Mohenjo-daro. These include:

Bronze statues such as the famous Dancing Girl, demonstrating advanced metallurgical skills and artistic expression. The statue's confident posture and elaborate jewelry suggest a society with sophisticated aesthetic sensibilities.

Terracotta figurines representing various human forms and possibly deities, providing evidence of religious beliefs and artistic traditions. These mass-produced items suggest standardized religious or cultural practices.

Gold and semi-precious stone jewelry indicating wealth and trade connections. The presence of carnelian from Maharashtra and lapis lazuli from Afghanistan demonstrates extensive trade networks.

Games and toys suggesting domestic life and children's activities. Dice, game boards, and terracotta animal figurines demonstrate leisure activities and domestic culture.

## Chronology Establishment

### Stratigraphic Analysis

The excavation layers at Mohenjo-daro revealed a complex sequence of occupations. Archaeologists identified three main periods:

Early Period: Characterized by relatively simple construction and smaller structures. Buildings from this period were often altered or incorporated into later constructions.

Mature Period: The golden age of Mohenjo-daro, when the city's most impressive structures were built. Standardized bricks, sophisticated drainage, and elaborate public buildings date to this period.

Late Period: Evidence of gradual decline, with structures showing signs of neglect and modification. The characteristic Harappan standardized artifacts become less common, replaced by simpler items.

### Radiocarbon Dating

Radiocarbon dating of organic materials from Mohenjo-daro, combined with cross-dating using Mesopotamian trade artifacts, established the site's chronology. The mature Harappan phase, including the construction of major structures, dates to approximately 2600-1900 BCE, with occupation beginning as early as 3300 BCE.

## Impact on Archaeological Understanding

The discovery of Mohenjo-daro fundamentally transformed understanding of ancient urban civilization. Before 1922, the Egyptian and Mesopotamian civilizations were considered the world's earliest urban cultures. The Harappan discovery demonstrated that sophisticated urban development occurred independently in South Asia, contemporaneous with these other civilizations.

The site also challenged prevailing theories about the necessary conditions for urban development. Unlike Egypt with its Nile or Mesopotamia with its Tigris-Euphrates, the Indus Valley lacked dramatic geographic features that might explain concentrated urban settlement. Understanding how this civilization developed has required new theoretical frameworks.

Finally, the discovery established a foundation for understanding regional cultural development. The Indus Valley Civilization represents a crucial link between early agricultural settlements and later historical civilizations of South Asia, providing context for understanding subsequent cultural developments on the subcontinent.
