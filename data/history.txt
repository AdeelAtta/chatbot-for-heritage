# History of Mohenjo-daro

## Overview of the Indus Valley Civilization

Mohenjo-daro, meaning "Mound of the Dead Men" in Sindhi, was one of the largest urban settlements of the ancient Indus Valley Civilization (also known as the Harappan Civilization). This Bronze Age civilization flourished in the northwestern regions of the Indian subcontinent, primarily in what is now Pakistan and northwest India, from approximately 3300 BCE to 1300 BCE.

The Indus Valley Civilization was one of the world's earliest urban cultures, rivaling ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia in its scale and sophistication. At its peak, the civilization covered an area larger than either contemporary civilization and maintained a high degree of cultural homogeneity across its vast territory spanning over 1 million square kilometers.

## Timeline and Chronology

### Early Harappan Phase (3300-2600 BCE)
The earliest traces of settlement at Mohenjo-daro date to around 3300 BCE. During this period, the settlement began as a small agricultural village and gradually grew into a planned urban center. Early inhabitants engaged in farming, pottery making, and basic trade networks with neighboring regions.

### Mature Harappan Phase (2600-1900 BCE)
This represents the golden age of Mohenjo-daro when the city reached its peak population of approximately 40,000 inhabitants. The city was meticulously planned with a sophisticated drainage system, standardized brick sizes, and a grid-like street pattern. Major structures including the Great Bath, the Granary, and the Citadel were constructed during this period.

The city served as a major administrative and commercial center, with evidence of extensive trade networks extending to Mesopotamia, the Persian Gulf, and possibly as far as the Mediterranean region. The inhabitants developed an undeciphered writing system that has yet to be fully decoded, with over 400 distinct symbols identified on various seals and tablets.

### Late Harappan Phase (1900-1300 BCE)
Around 1900 BCE, Mohenjo-daro and other major Harappan cities began to show signs of decline. Archaeological evidence suggests a gradual abandonment rather than a sudden catastrophe. The population likely dispersed to smaller settlements, and the sophisticated urban infrastructure fell into disuse.

## Geographic Context

Mohenjo-daro is located in the Sindh province of modern-day Pakistan, situated on the right bank of the Indus River. The site lies approximately 28 kilometers from the town of Sukkur and sits on elevated ground that rises above the surrounding floodplain. The strategic location along the river made it a crucial hub for trade and agricultural activities.

The ancient city was built on a massive earthen mound and covered an area of approximately 250 hectares, making it one of the largest urban centers of its time. The deliberate selection of an elevated site offered protection against annual flooding, a common phenomenon along the Indus River valley.

## Daily Life in Mohenjo-daro

### Population and Social Structure
Archaeological evidence suggests Mohenjo-daro housed between 30,000 to 40,000 inhabitants at its peak. The society appears to have been relatively egalitarian compared to contemporary civilizations, with no clear evidence of monumental royal tombs or elaborate palaces. This has led scholars to speculate about a more collective form of governance or a society organized around functional roles rather than hereditary aristocracy.

The city was divided into two main sections: the Citadel in the west and the Lower City in the east. The Citadel contained significant public buildings including the Great Bath, the Granary, and what may have been a college or assembly hall. The Lower City housed residential dwellings, workshops, and markets.

### Economy and Livelihoods
The inhabitants of Mohenjo-daro engaged in diverse occupations including farming, craft production, and trade. Agricultural activities centered around wheat, barley, and cotton cultivation, supported by an intricate irrigation system that harnessed the waters of the Indus and its tributaries.

Craft specialization was highly developed, with evidence of skilled workers producing beads, ceramics, textiles, and metal goods. The standardization of weights and measures across the civilization facilitated fair trade and commerce, both within the Harappan territories and with external trading partners.

### Housing and Domestic Life
Residential buildings in Mohenjo-daro typically followed a standard plan, with rooms arranged around a central courtyard. Most houses featured private wells for water access and private bathrooms with connections to the public drainage system. This attention to sanitation was remarkable for the period and demonstrates the advanced civic planning of the civilization.

Houses were constructed using standardized mud bricks, with consistent dimensions of approximately 40 x 20 x 10 centimeters. The uniformity in building materials suggests centralized production and distribution systems managed by civic authorities.

## Decline and Abandonment

### Theories of Decline
The decline and eventual abandonment of Mohenjo-daro has been the subject of considerable scholarly debate. Multiple factors likely contributed to the collapse of this urban civilization:

1. **Climate Change**: Evidence suggests a significant aridification event around 2200-1900 BCE, which may have reduced agricultural productivity and forced population relocation to more water-secure regions.

2. **River Diversion**: The Indus River may have shifted course over time, depriving the city of its crucial water source and trade route. Some geomorphological studies suggest the river's channel moved eastward over millennia.

3. **Ecological Degradation**: Deforestation, soil exhaustion, and the spread of salinity may have degraded the agricultural base that sustained the urban population.

4. **Epidemic or Plague**: Some researchers have suggested disease may have contributed to population decline, though direct evidence remains limited.

5. **External Invasions**: Earlier theories proposing Aryan invasions have largely been discredited in favor of endogenous decline models, as the archaeological record shows no evidence of violent destruction.

### The Final Days
Archaeological layers indicate a gradual abandonment rather than a sudden end. Buildings were not destroyed violently but appear to have been slowly vacated over generations. The last inhabitants may have been relatively poor, occupying spaces originally designed for more affluent households, suggesting a stratified exodus as conditions deteriorated.

By approximately 1300 BCE, Mohenjo-daro appears to have been largely abandoned, with vegetation gradually reclaiming the monumental structures. The city would remain forgotten until its rediscovery in the 19th century.

## Historical Significance

Mohenjo-daro stands as testament to human ingenuity and urban planning capabilities more than 4,000 years ago. Its sophisticated drainage systems, standardized building materials, and planned urban layout influenced architectural and civic planning traditions for millennia.

The site represents a unique chapter in human history where a complex civilization emerged without the typical hallmarks of divine kingship or monumental religious architecture found in Egypt or Mesopotamia. Understanding how this society functioned provides valuable insights into alternative models of urban governance and social organization.

The archaeological evidence from Mohenjo-daro continues to challenge our assumptions about ancient societies and reminds us that the trajectory of human civilization is not predetermined but shaped by environmental, social, and technological factors that remain relevant to contemporary discussions about urban sustainability and resilience.
